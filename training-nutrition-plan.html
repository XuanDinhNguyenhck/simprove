How the hour works

Same shape every gym day. The 10-minute run is a warm-up, not a workout — keep it at a pace where you could hold a conversation. Trying to make it a fat-burning session will only steal energy from the lifting that actually changes your body.

0–10 minEasy run

Conversational pace. Raises core temperature and gets blood into the muscles.

10–13 minPrep

Shoulder and hip mobility, then two light sets of the first exercise.

13–52 minLifting

90 s rest on compounds, 60 s on isolation. Log every set.

52–60 minCore & stretch

Finish the core work, then stretch hip flexors, chest and hamstrings.

Leave 1–2 reps in reserve on most sets. Only the final set of an isolation exercise should go close to failure. Each week, add one rep or 2.5 kg — when you hit the top of the rep range on every set, increase the weight and drop back to the bottom.

The four sessions

Upper body gets three of the four days because that is your priority and because football already trains your legs hard twice a week. Every day is six exercises or fewer, which is what keeps each session inside the hour with proper rest between sets. Each card shows how long that exercise takes including its rest periods, so you can see exactly where your hour goes. Rest periods are between sets, not between exercises — the rest after your final set is also your walk to the next station, so you never take an extra one. Where two exercises are marked as a superset, do them back to back with about 15 seconds between them, then take the full rest before the next round — never superset the heavy compounds at the start of a session. Eating

At 59 kg your maintenance works out near 2,325 kcal a day averaged across the week. Calories move with your training load rather than staying flat, so you are fully fuelled on match days and lightest on the rest day. The weekly average lands about 50 kcal under maintenance — that is close to maintenance on purpose. Your BMI is 22.2, so the job now is recomposition, not losing more weight: an aggressive deficit here costs you muscle and match performance long before it costs you fat.

Tracking

Six things. Everything else is noise.

  1. Weigh in once a week

    Sunday morning, after the toilet, before eating or drinking. Compare weekly averages only — day-to-day numbers are mostly water.

  2. Target: flat or −0.2 to −0.4 kg per week

    While your lifts go up. That combination is recomposition working. Losing faster than 0.5 kg a week means you're burning muscle too.

  3. Adjust with 150 kcal steps

    Dropping too fast? Add 150. Nothing moved in three weeks and the tape hasn't shifted? Subtract 150. Never more than that at once.

  4. Measure your waist every two weeks

    At the navel, first thing in the morning. In a recomposition the tape moves before the scale does — this is the number that tells you the belly is changing.

  5. Log every single set

    Weight, reps, and how hard it felt. Progressive overload is the entire mechanism of muscle growth. If the numbers in your log aren't climbing, nothing else you do matters.

  6. Sleep 8 hours and drink 2.5–3 L

    Six training days a week only works if you recover. Under-sleeping blunts muscle growth and makes fat loss harder at the same time.

Notes

The honest caveats

You cannot burn fat from one specific place. The core work here builds strength and posture; the belly changes when your total body fat drops. At your weight, a good part of what you are seeing is abdominal wall tone rather than fat — getting your squat and Romanian deadlift strong will change how your midsection looks more than any diet tweak.

The calorie numbers are estimates from a formula, not measurements, and they assume you are male and roughly 18–22. The formula itself carries about ±10%, which is ±230 kcal here — larger than the deficit it is being used to set. Treat the first three weeks as calibration: adjust on what the scale and the tape measure actually do, not on what the formula predicted. Recalculate these numbers if your weight moves more than about 3 kg.

I am not a dietitian or a doctor. If you have any medical condition, or you feel persistently exhausted, dizzy or run-down once this starts, speak to a GP or a sports dietitian before pushing on.

Where the numbers come from

Energy comes from Mifflin–St Jeor: at 163 cm and 59 kg your resting rate is about 1,514 kcal, times 1.30 for ordinary daily movement, plus roughly 270 kcal for a gym hour and 710 kcal for a 90-minute match. Protein at 2.2 g/kg bodyweight — about 2.5 g/kg of fat-free mass — sits above the 1.6 g/kg point where added protein stops helping muscle gain, and inside the higher band recommended for lean athletes eating at a deficit. Cycling carbohydrate with training load follows football nutrition guidance, which puts intake at 6–8 g/kg around a match and 3–5 g/kg on lighter days. Fat is held at 1 g/kg as a floor, not a target. The deliberately slow rate of loss comes from a trial in elite athletes: the group losing 0.7% of bodyweight per week gained 2.1% lean mass, while the group losing 1.4% per week gained none.
